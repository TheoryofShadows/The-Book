The texts are public domain — the World English Bible with Deuterocanon, R. H. Charles's 1917 Enoch and Jubilees, and the Ante-Nicene Fathers of 1870 and 1885. Nothing here is drawn from a modern copyrighted translation.

The reference data is openly licensed, not public domain. Word definitions are Easton's Bible Dictionary, 1897, itself public domain, in a machine-readable parse by NEUU under CC BY 4.0. Coordinates come from OpenBible.info's Bible Geocoding under CC BY 4.0, parts of it derived from OpenStreetMap under the ODbL.

The order is a scholarly reconstruction and is contested at many points. The accuracy report sets out what was verified, what was corrected, and what is still missing.

Every verse number is a button: reach it with Tab and press Enter to save the verse, copy it, or copy a link to it. Text size is adjustable in any chapter, the whole site works in light and dark, and animation is dropped if your system asks for reduced motion. On a phone every link sits on screen rather than behind a sideways swipe, and the bar carrying them steps out of the way as you read down the page, returning as soon as you scroll back up.

Any chapter can be read aloud. The narration uses the voices already installed on your device — nothing is downloaded and no text leaves the page — so the range and quality of the voices is whatever your system provides, and the reading is a synthetic one, not a performance. It announces the chapter, follows the verse being spoken, keeps its place when you leave, and runs on from chapter to chapter and from one work into the next one written, unless you turn that off. That sequence is the order of composition, not the printed order. Browsers with no speech engine simply do not show the control. On a phone the reading usually stops when the screen locks or you change app: the browser suspends the page, and this is a voice speaking the page rather than an audio file playing behind it.
